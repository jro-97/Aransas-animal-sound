CANDIDATE #1

Ring-Tailed Lemur

Lemur catta
The Neighbor's Pet · Most Likely
85%
Acoustic Match
▼

✅ Evidence FOR

  • The neighbor literally owns pet lemurs — confirmed
  • Ring-tailed lemur "moan" contact call: short, high-pitched, tonal — used when separated from group
  • Used during travel and vigilance — exactly what an outdoor or escaped pet would do at night
  • 1040 Hz fundamental, 200–400 ms duration, flat contour all align with lemur moan characteristics
  • Frequency range consistent with primate contact vocalizations

❌ Evidence AGAINST

  • Can't confirm the lemurs were outside that specific night
  • Could be another species that happens to match acoustically

🎵 Typical Vocalization

Moan call at 800–1200 Hz, short duration (200–400 ms), flat tonal note. Specifically a contact call: "I'm here — where are you?"

🌟 Wild Card Ring-tailed lemurs have 28+ distinct vocalizations — more than most primates. Their "moan" specifically means "I'm here, where are you?" — exactly what a disoriented or separated pet would broadcast into the night.

Jaguarundi

Herpailurus yagouaroundi
The Ghost Cat · Strongest Unconventional
90%
Acoustic Match
▼

✅ Evidence FOR

  • Has 13 vocalizations including a "peculiar bird-like chirp" unlike any other feline
  • People consistently mistake jaguarundi sounds for birds — fits "doesn't sound like any bird I know"
  • Ground-dwelling, crepuscular/nocturnal hunter — fits the observation perfectly

🎵 Typical Vocalization

Bird-like chirp at 900–1200 Hz, short tonal pulses. Used as a contact call. Field observers frequently mistake it for a bird — not a cat.

🌟 Wild Card Jaguarundis are the only wild cat in the Western Hemisphere with no spots or stripes. They look like a weasel crossed with a small panther and were kept as rodent-control pets by pre-Columbian peoples. If this is a jaguarundi, it could be the first Texas evidence in 40 years.

CANDIDATE #3

Kinkajou

Potos flavus
La Llorona
70%
Acoustic Match
▼

✅ Evidence FOR

  • Legal exotic pet in Texas — strictly nocturnal
  • Highly vocal with screams, barks, and tonal "weedle" calls
  • Nicknamed la llorona (The Crying Woman) in Central America for eerie nighttime cries
  • Two-part call includes a series of tonal weedles that could produce a 1040 Hz signature
  • Escaped kinkajous documented in Florida and Texas
  • Originally classified as Lemur flavus by early scientists — an ironic connection

❌ Evidence AGAINST

  • Weedle calls are typically more frequency-modulated than our flat-contour mystery call
  • Would need to be an escaped pet — no wild population in Texas
  • Calls tend to be louder and more varied than what's in the recording

🎵 Typical Vocalization

Tonal "weedle" series at 800–1500 Hz, often with frequency sweeps. Also produces loud screams and soft chirps.

🌟 Wild Card Kinkajous are in the raccoon family but look like monkeys, live in trees, and have prehensile tails. When first discovered, scientists were so confused they classified them as a lemur species (Lemur flavus). This overlap with our top candidate is not a coincidence we can ignore.

🌟 Wild Card Pauraques are virtually invisible on the ground — their mottled feathers look exactly like dead leaves. Their eyes glow bright orange in flashlight beams, which is usually how people discover them sitting right at their feet.
